\begin{abstract}
In this study, we integrate machine learning and computational chemistry to elucidate reactivity and substituent effects in hypervalent iodine compounds. Detailed DFT descriptors, combined with structural fingerprints and Mordred-derived features, enabled the development of a robust categorization scheme for IBX derivatives based on the number and positions of their substituents. Our models reveal that, consistent with previous literature, steric factors dominate reactivity: Bulky groups in the ortho position relative to iodine significantly increase the dihedral angle ($\Theta$), which in turn lowers the transition state barrier and enhances reactivity. Furthermore, additional categories capture multi-substituted compounds, highlighting distinct profiles—such as those exhibiting a buttressing effect, where the addition of a second bulky group further amplifies molecular twisting. SHAP analyses corroborate these insights by identifying the $\Theta$ and dipole moments as key determinants of reactivity. Collectively, our findings provide mechanistic insights and a predictive framework for the rational design of more efficient and selective hypervalent iodine reagents. 


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{introduction}
Hypervalent iodine compounds have established themselves as essential tools in organic chemistry, offering efficient and environmentally friendly alternatives to heavy metal reagents for diverse synthetic transformations. These compounds, particularly iodine(III) and iodine(V) derivatives, have found widespread application as selective oxidizing agents and versatile reagents for functionalizing complex organic molecules. Their unique chemical properties, characterized by reactivity and selectivity,  positioned them as indispensable in a variety of oxidative and bond-forming reactions~\cite{zhdankin2021hypervalent}. As the field has evolved, hypervalent iodine chemistry  extensively explored, with numerous reviews and comprehensive studies highlighting the broad utility and potential of these compounds in organic synthesis. Hypervalent iodine(III) and iodine(V) species attached to a benzene ring are widely used in organic synthesis. Among these compounds, those incorporated into five-membered rings—such as IBX 1 and IBA 2 (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig1}A))—are the most well-known~\cite{zhdankin2009hypervalent,ladziata2006hypervalent}.

The reactivity of hypervalent iodine compounds is profoundly influenced by the ligands coordinated to the iodine center and the substituents on the aryl ring. For instance, iodine(III) reagents are used in various ligand exchange reactions and catalytic oxidation reactions~\cite{yoshimura2016advances}. Strong trans ligands confer lability, whereas combinations of strong and weak, or weak and weak, ligands enhance stability, thereby affecting reactivity~\cite{zhdankin2009hypervalent}. This ligand-dependent behavior is exemplified by diaryliodonium salts 3 ( ~\ref{fig:fig1}A), in which electron-deficient aryl groups are preferentially transferred during reactions, highlighting the significant role of electronic properties in guiding transformations~\cite{olofsson2016arylation}.

Regarding iodine(V) reagents, they are employed in various controlled oxidation reactions, including catalytic or stoichiometric processes, such as the oxidation of alcohols~\cite{de1996hypervalent}, amines, sulfides~\cite{nicolaou2004iodoxybenzoic}, aromatics~\cite{pouysegu2010hypervalent,zhang2023metal}, and heteroaromatics~\cite{howard2013controlled, farshadfar2022discovery}. The main barrier to their widespread use is the insolubility of IBX in most organic solvents. Moreover, most of these hypervalent iodine compounds are not sufficiently strong for some reactions. Dess–Martin periodinane (DMP) (4) (~\ref{fig:fig1}A)is widely used for the mild and selective oxidation of alcohols to carbonyl compounds and is soluble in most organic solvents~\cite{ireland1993improved}. In fact, the performance of DMP is enhanced by its solubility and the assistance of Brønsted acids~\cite{satam20102}.

 Su and Goddard illustrate a mechanism using DFT calculations, showing that the twisting energy of hypervalent iodine(V) is the rate-determining step (RDS) in the oxidation of methanol. They noted that ligand exchange occurs rapidly, with a low energy barrier. They further found that this twisting is essential for the subsequent step, which involves either the elimination of alcohol or the cleavage of the I–OR bond. Moreover, they observed that alkyl groups ortho to the iodoxy group enhance twisting; however, as these substituents increase in size, steric effects shift the rate-determining step to ligand exchange, with o‑tBuIBX exhibiting the poorest ligand exchange~\cite{su2005enhancing} (see Fig~\ref{fig:fig1}B and C).

Although Schaefer explained that DFT computations with larger basis sets reveal that reductive elimination involving C–H bond cleavage is the rate-determining step (RDS)\cite{jiang2017twist}, for larger alcohols the RDS is the twisting step\cite{parida2023lambda3} or even ligand exchange. This is because larger alcohols require more energy to overcome the twisting barrier while needing less energy for the elimination step compared to methanol~\cite{jiang2017twist}. To complete this investigation, we also provide a mechanism demonstrating that hypervalent twisting is necessary for both ligand exchange and elimination steps in the oxidation of alcohols and amines using IBX reagent~\cite{chipman2019dft}.

Water plays a critical role in the oxidation of alcohols by IBX. The process involves a two-step mechanism in which water assists in proton transfer, thereby facilitating the conversion of the alcohol ligand. The presence of water, coupled with hypervalent twisting, leads to the formation of a twisted intermediate that eventually undergoes disproportionation. This synergistic interaction between water and the IBX reagent accelerates the transformation, enhancing the overall reactivity of the system~\cite{su2005enhancing}.

Lewis acids, such as BF3, and boron-containing compounds significantly influence IBX-mediated alcohol oxidation. These acids facilitate the ligand exchange process, thereby reducing the energy barrier for the oxidation step. They stabilize the intermediate transition state, leading to more efficient and rapid oxidation. Furthermore, the presence of these acids alters the reactivity profile by favoring certain substrates and promoting the formation of desired products with higher yield and selectivity~\cite{ochiai2006trans,jiang2017twist}. Brønsted acids, such as AcOH and TFA, can also increase the solubility and reactivity of iodine hypervalent compounds in various reactions~\cite{tojo2006oxidation}.For example, IBX with catalytic TsOH·H2O can perform chemoselective one-pot cleavage and oxidation of silyl ethers in good yields, in the presence of various protecting groups~\cite{kamo2024chemoselective}.

The reactivity of hypervalent iodine compounds is significantly influenced by replacing hydroxy ligands with acidic groups, such as OAc in DMP~\cite{de1996hypervalent}, OTs (5), and OMs (6)\cite{yusubov20132}(~\ref{fig:fig1}A). This substitution not only improves solubility in common organic solvents but is also essential for reactions that require a lower energy barrier at the RDS~\cite{farshadfar2022discovery}.


Another type of IBX is sudo-cyclic IBX derivatives, such as IBS (7)~\cite{zhdankin2005derivatives}, amide (8)~\cite{zhdankin2003ibx}, and ester (9)~\cite{hitomi2014synthesis}iodine hypervalent reagents, which are modified versions of IBX where the iodine center is attached to different functional groups. These modifications can influence their reactivity and solubility, offering enhanced chemoselectivity in oxidation reactions. Ester and amide IBX derivatives typically exhibit improved solubility compared to standard IBX, making them useful in various synthetic applications~\cite{zhdankin2004preparation}. However, most of these compounds are expensive, and the synthesis of some of them is complex.

Substituent effects, whether steric, electronic, or both, can significantly influence reactivity, as demonstrated in Goddard's study~\cite{parida2023lambda3,su2005enhancing}. Studies showed that ortho-substituents on iodine(V) compounds, such as IBX, play a pivotal role in facilitating key mechanistic steps. Alkyl groups promote the twisting step, enabling faster reaction rates compared to unsubstituted IBX~\cite{moorthy2011twist}. For instance, Me-IBX (10), a methyl-substituted derivative of IBX, exhibits enhanced reactivity, thereby expanding its utility in various oxidative transformations~\cite{moorthy2011twist}. However, in iodine(V) hypervalent compounds, functional groups attached at different positions on the aromatic ring often exhibit both steric and electronic effects~\cite{parida2023lambda3,su2005enhancing,moorthy2008modified}, which may either be synergistic or antagonistic. In some cases, the resulting properties cannot be accurately predicted without synthesizing the reagents or performing comprehensive computational studies~\cite{su2005enhancing}.\par

However, despite extensive empirical evidence and various mechanistic studies, the rate-determining step (RDS) in alcohol oxidation is not unique—it can vary depending on the alcohol or the type of IBX used. Importantly, twisting is crucial for both ligand exchange and elimination. Computational chemistry has proven invaluable in elucidating these mechanisms, as demonstrated by studies showing how twisting processes and ortho-substituents influence reaction rates in transformations such as alcohol oxidation~\cite{su2005enhancing, moorthy2011twist}. This synergy of experimental and theoretical approaches provides a deeper understanding of the nuanced reactivity of these versatile reagents.

While several comprehensive review articles published in recent years covering DFT and experimental studies~\cite{parida2023lambda3, zhdankin2011organoiodine, shetgaonkar2023iodine,zhdankin2013hypervalent} some factors influencing reactivity remain unknown due to the limited data of different IBX substituents, both theoretically and experimentally. In this work, we selected various IBX derivatives and employed DFT calculations to compute the transition state barrier for the twisting step in alcohol oxidation, aiming to identify new reactivity features using machine learning methods.\par

Machine learning (ML) has become a valuable tool for predicting the behavior of reagents in chemical reactions by analyzing the structural effects of substituents. It allows researchers to uncover patterns and trends from extensive datasets of experimental and computational results, enabling the rational design of reagents with reduced reliance on exhaustive synthesis~\cite{artrith2021best}. Among ML techniques, neural networks (NN)  stand out for their ability to handle complex and nonlinear relationships, making them particularly suited for modeling the intricate interplay of substituents’ effects on reactivity~\cite{wei2016neural}. However, the effectiveness of ML, and NN in particular, is constrained by the availability and quality of datasets, as limited or biased data can undermine the accuracy of predictions. Furthermore, the "black box" nature of NN complicates the extraction of mechanistic insights, necessitating careful interpretation and validation~\cite{benitez1997artificial}. Relying on both ML-based predictions and traditional experimental approaches is ideal, as it combines the efficiency of data-driven tools with the reliability of established methods to advance the design of hypervalent iodine reagents~\cite{shwartz2017opening}
.\par

\section{Materials and methods}
\subsection{Computational Details}
Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to evaluate the transition state (TS) barriers for the twisting mechanism in methanol oxidation, following the methodology established by Goddard et al~\cite{su2005enhancing}. (see Figure ~\ref{fig:fig1}B). All calculations were conducted using the Gaussian 16 software package~\cite{tomberg2013gaussian}. These computations encompassed 284 IBX derivatives. Geometry optimizations and frequency analyses were carried out at the M06-2X level of theory~\cite{hohenstein2008assessment}, employing the SMD solvation model with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) as the solvent. Transition states for methanol oxidation reactions were identified using the Berny algorithm, and the corresponding calculations were confirmed by frequency analyses, ensuring the presence of a single imaginary frequency corresponding to the reaction coordinate.

For IBX derivatives, the Stuttgart/Dresden (SDD) effective core potential (ECP) was applied to iodine, while carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, chlorine, phosphorus, fluorine, sulfur, and nitrogen atoms were described using the 6-31G(d) basis set. Single-point energy calculations were subsequently performed on the optimized structures using the M06-2X functional with the def2-TZVP basis set for all atoms, including iodine, within the same SMD solvation model (DMSO). The distribution of TS barriers for the twisting mechanism is depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:fig2}B).

\subsection{Machine Learning Models}

A comprehensive predictive framework for estimating the transition state (TS) barrier was developed through the application of several machine learning (ML) models. The model selection was guided by each algorithm’s capacity to capture nonlinear relationships, manage high-dimensional descriptor spaces, and yield robust generalization. The algorithms considered in this study include Linear Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting (GB), Support Vector Regression (SVR), and Neural Networks (NN). A baseline LR model was first implemented to serve as a point of reference due to its simplicity, interpretability, and computational efficiency. However, given the complexity of chemical systems and the inherent nonlinearity of molecular interactions, more sophisticated models were subsequently explored.These models were trained and evaluated using rigorous statistical techniques to determine their predictive performance, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:fig3}.

\subsubsection{Linear Regression (LR)}
Linear regression models the relationship between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables through a linear equation. In this study, LR was used to relate the TS barrier to molecular descriptors derived from density functional theory (DFT) optimizations, Mordred descriptors, and Morgan fingerprints. The model follows the standard ordinary least squares (OLS) formulation:
\begin{equation}
    y = \beta_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{n} \beta_i x_i + \epsilon,
\end{equation}
where \(y\) represents the TS barrier, \(x_i\) are the molecular descriptors, \(\beta_i\) are the regression coefficients, \(\beta_0\) is the intercept, and \(\epsilon\) is the error term. Model training was performed by minimizing the mean squared error (MSE) without the inclusion of regularization techniques, thus ensuring a pure linear approach~\cite{hope2020linear, reback2020pandas,das2004mean}. As an example of this use in chemistry, LR models  applied in predicting adsorption energies of molecules on catalyst surfaces using appropriate metal descriptors, enabling efficient catalyst screening and material design~\cite{chowdhury2018prediction}.

\subsubsection{Random Forest (RF)}
Random Forest is an ensemble learning method that constructs multiple decision trees and aggregates their predictions to reduce overfitting and enhance accuracy. In this study, RF was employed to capture both linear and nonlinear interactions among descriptors. Each tree in the forest is built on a bootstrap sample of the training data, and a random subset of features is used for splitting at each node. The final prediction is the average of individual tree predictions:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} h_t(X),
\end{equation}
where \(T\) is the number of trees and \(h_t(X)\) is the prediction from the \(t\)-th tree ~\cite{breiman2001random,breiman2001random,pedregosa2011scikit}. Shi et al. employed random forest models to predict chemopreventive agents, screening 23,000 compounds and selecting 18 validated leads, highlighting the model's effectiveness in chemoprevention.~\cite{sprague2014design}.


\subsubsection\{Gradient Boosting (GB)} 
Gradient Boosting is an iterative ensemble technique that sequentially trains weak learners (typically decision trees) to correct the errors of preceding models. Each iteration minimizes the residual error by adding a new model to the ensemble: \textbackslash{}begin\{equation\} \textbackslash{}hat\{y\}\_i = F\_M(X\_i) = F\_\{M-1\}(X\_i) + \textbackslash{}gamma\_M h\_M(X\_i), \textbackslash{}end\{equation\} where \textbackslash{}( \textbackslash{}gamma\_M \textbackslash{}) is the learning rate and \textbackslash{}( h\_M(X\_i) \textbackslash{}) is the \textbackslash{}(M\textbackslash{})-th base learner. This approach facilitates the modeling of complex relationships by iteratively refining predictions ~\cite{friedman2001greedy, friedman2001greedy }.Sheridan demonstrated that XGBoost is an effective and efficient QSAR modeling method, providing accurate predictions with minimal computation. It handles large datasets, supports frequent updates, and benefits from prediction rescaling. Despite sensitivity to noise and lacking a domain applicability metric, it remains a powerful tool in pharmaceutical applications~\cite{sheridan2016extreme}.  

\subsubsection{Support Vector Regression (SVR)}
Support Vector Regression extends support vector machines to regression tasks by identifying a hyperplane that best fits the data while allowing a specified margin of tolerance. The SVR model is formulated by minimizing:
\begin{equation}
    \min_{w,b} \frac{1}{2} \|w\|^2,
\end{equation}
subject to:
\begin{equation}
    |y_i - (w^T x_i + b)| \leq \epsilon,
\end{equation}
where \(w\) is the weight vector, \(b\) is the bias term, and \(\epsilon\) defines the width of the \(\epsilon\)-insensitive tube. To capture nonlinear dependencies, an RBF kernel was employed:
\begin{equation}
    K(x_i, x_j) = \exp\left(-\gamma \|x_i - x_j\|^2\right),
\end{equation}
with \(\gamma\) as the kernel hyperparameter ~\cite{drucker1996support, liu2011feature, willmott2005advantages }. Hong et al. integrated chemical knowledge into ML for reaction performance prediction, employing support vector regression (SVR), quantum computations, and transfer learning. Their review highlights AI’s potential to enhance synthetic chemistry while addressing challenges in catalyst design and reaction optimization~\cite{zhang2023bridging}.

\subsubsection{Neural Networks (NN)}
Artificial neural networks, particularly feedforward networks, are computational models inspired by the structure and function of biological neural systems. They consist of layers of interconnected nodes (neurons), each learning progressively abstract representations of the input data. Neural network models, especially those based on deep learning, offer high flexibility and superior performance for modeling complex, high-dimensional data, although they typically require significant computational resources and large training datasets. Moreover, NN models tend to be less interpretable compared to more conventional methods such as LR or decision trees~\cite{abdi1999neural,bebis1994feed}. Deep neural networks have been successfully applied in computational chemistry. For instance, Goh \textit{et al.} demonstrated that deep neural networks (DNNs) can learn features directly from raw data, thereby enabling accurate predictions of physicochemical properties. These models have found broad applications in areas including drug design, structural biology, quantum chemistry, and materials science~\cite{goh2017deep}.

A \textit{multi-layer perceptron} (MLP) regressor was implemented, consisting of an input layer, two hidden layers, and an output layer. Each layer transforms the input via weighted summations and nonlinear activations. The forward propagation in the network is given by:
\begin{equation}
    a^{(l)} = \sigma(W^{(l)} a^{(l-1)} + b^{(l)}),
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \( W^{(l)} \) is the weight matrix for layer \( l \),
    \item \( b^{(l)} \) is the bias vector,
    \item \( \sigma \) is an activation function (e.g., ReLU, tanh),
    \item \( a^{(l)} \) represents the activation at layer \( l \).
\end{itemize}
The hidden layers, configured with 100 and 50 neurons, employ the ReLU activation function:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma(z) = \max(0, z),
\end{equation}
and the output layer utilizes a linear activation to yield continuous predictions for the TS barrier.

Training was performed using the Adam optimizer, which adaptively adjusts the learning rate during training. The model was optimized by minimizing the mean squared error (MSE) loss:
\begin{equation}
    \text{MSE} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2,
\end{equation}
with weight updates computed via backpropagation:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(l)}} = \delta^{(l)} \, a^{(l-1)T},
\end{equation}
where \(\delta^{(l)}\) is the backpropagated error term. Training was conducted over a maximum of 1000 iterations using mini-batches of 32 samples, with early stopping implemented to avoid overfitting.

\paragraph{Evaluation Protocol}  
All models were trained on 80\% of the dataset and evaluated on the remaining 20\%. A 5-fold cross-validation procedure was applied to mitigate selection bias. The performance of each model was quantified using metrics such as root mean square error (RMSE) and the coefficient of determination (\(R^2\)). Residual analysis and parity plots were generated to assess prediction accuracy and error distribution, ensuring the reliability of the predictive framework.

This systematic approach to model selection and evaluation allowed for the identification of the most suitable ML algorithm for TS barrier prediction, forming a robust foundation for further refinement and application in computational chemistry.

\subsubsection{\subsubsection{Hyperparameter Tuning and Model Evaluation}

Hyperparameter tuning was undertaken for Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting (GB), Support Vector Regression (SVR), and Neural Networks (NN) to enhance generalization and predictive performance. A systematic exploration of the hyperparameter spaces was performed using GridSearchCV for ensemble-based models and RandomizedSearchCV for SVR and NN models. Model performance was quantified using metrics such as root mean square error (RMSE) and the coefficient of determination (\(R^2\)), while residual analyses were conducted to assess prediction quality.

For ensemble models (RF and GB), key hyperparameters—including the number of trees (\(n\_estimators\)), maximum tree depth (\(max\_depth\)), and minimum samples per split (\(min\_samples\_split\))—were optimized to balance model complexity with variance reduction. Similarly, GB hyperparameters, such as the learning rate (\(learning\_rate\)) and the number of boosting stages, were fine-tuned to improve convergence and mitigate overfitting.

In the case of SVR, tuning was focused on the regularization parameter (\(C\)), kernel type (linear or radial basis function), and the epsilon (\(\varepsilon\)) threshold to achieve an optimal bias–variance trade-off. For NN models, parameters such as the hidden layer configuration, activation functions (e.g., ReLU, tanh), solver methods (e.g., Adam, LBFGS), and learning rate schedules were adjusted to enhance training stability and convergence. Early stopping was implemented and a maximum iteration limit was enforced to address the sensitivity of neural networks to initialization and training dynamics.

A 5-fold cross-validation procedure was employed to obtain statistically robust performance estimates. In addition, learning curves were generated to evaluate generalization across varying training set sizes, while residual and quantile-quantile (QQ) plots were analyzed to verify the normality and homoscedasticity of prediction errors.

The optimal model was selected based on achieving the highest \(R^2\) and the lowest RMSE. Subsequent interpretability analyses, including SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Partial Dependence Plots (PDPs), were performed on the best-performing model to elucidate feature importance and the influence of key descriptors. Finally, the selected model was persisted using joblib, ensuring reproducibility and facilitating predictions on unseen molecular structures.

\subsection{Data set}
\subsubsection{DFT descriptors}

The dataset~\ref{s} was generated from a DFT optimization of IBX structures and includes 25 descriptors categorized into energy and geometry. Eight key descriptors were chosen from this dataset for training various ML models: HOMO, LUMO, Dipole Moment X ($\vec{\mu}_x$), Dipole Moment Total ($\vec{\mu}$), Dihedral ($\Theta$), and the bond lengths of I–O, I=O, and I–OH.$\vec{\mu}_x$ represents the component of the molecular dipole moment along the x-axis, indicating charge separation in this direction~\cite{housecroft2008inorganic}. It provides insight into the electronic distribution and polarity of the IBX structures. The target variable, TS Barrier, represents the transition state barrier for twisting in methanol oxidation. Notably, the $\Theta$, which is a dihedral angle as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:fig2}A, was first reported by Goddard~\cite{su2005enhancing} to correlate with lower TS barriers in IBX derivatives having higher $\Theta$, and Moorthy confirmed this in a study that combined DFT and experimental catalysis~\cite{moorthy2011twist}. Additionally, the iodine–oxygen bond parameters are depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:fig2}A.

\subsubsection{Mordred  descriptors}

Mordred enables the conversion of molecular structures into a comprehensive set of numerical descriptors that encapsulate various physicochemical and structural properties. The resulting dataset comprises numerous descriptors relevant to the analysis of the transition state barrier (TS Barrier) and other related targets, as described in the Fingerprint Descriptors section~\cite{moriwaki2018mordred}. These descriptors serve as essential features for subsequent quantitative analyses and machine learning applications.

\subsubsection{Fingerprint descriptors}

Morgan fingerprints, also known as circular fingerprints, encode the presence of molecular substructures within a fixed-length binary vector, capturing essential structural information relevant to various chemical properties~\cite{pattanaik2020molecular}. In this study, fingerprints with 8192 bits and a radius of 2 were generated to ensure a comprehensive representation of molecular features. The resulting dataset consists of binary vectors corresponding to each molecule in the original dataset, along with associated target properties, including TS Barrier, HOMO, LUMO, various dipole moments, and geometric descriptors ($\Theta$, I–O, I=O, and I–OH bond lengths). These binary fingerprints serve as fundamental features for subsequent correlation analyses and machine learning model development in a non-numeric way, providing useful information about the DFT descriptors~\cite{aman2025prediction}.



\section{Results and discussion}
\subsection {Distribution of DFT descriptors}

A heatmap is generated using Pearson correlation to visualize the correlation coefficients between all numeric features that show a reasonable correlation with the TS barrier. This figure~\ref{fig:fig4} highlights strongly correlated features, where values close to ±1 indicate a strong linear relationship. Highly correlated predictors can introduce issues with multicollinearity, where redundant information between features may distort model interpretations and inflate variance in regression coefficients. Identifying and addressing these correlations—such as through feature selection or dimensionality reduction—can improve model stability. Additionally, a histogram overlaid with a kernel density estimate (KDE) is plotted to illustrate the distribution of the target variable (TS Barrier) and the DFT descriptors. This figure~\ref{fig:fig2}C-J aids in understanding the overall spread and central tendency of the selected descriptors in the dataset.\par

The distribution of the features in the dataset was thoroughly examined using both box plots and descriptive statistics. The analysis revealed varying degrees of variability among the features, with some displaying stable distributions and others showing significant fluctuations. The TS Barrier, for instance, exhibited a relatively wide distribution, with values ranging from ~9 to ~23 kcal·mol$^{-1}$. The mean value of 18.45 kcal·mol$^{-1}$, coupled with a standard deviation of $\Theta$=3.37   (IBX), suggests that while the TS barrier is generally concentrated around this central value, there is considerable variability. This spread in TS barrier values highlights the complexity of the transition state energies and their sensitivity to underlying molecular descriptors.\par

In contrast, features such as HOMO  and I-O Bond displayed much narrower distributions. The HOMO  values, with a mean of -0.30 and a standard deviation of just 0.02, indicated that this feature is tightly clustered around the central value, with minimal variation. The I-O Bond feature also showed a narrow range, with values between 2.11 and 2.21, reflecting a stable, consistent characteristic across the dataset. The low variability in these features suggests that they are reliable and may provide stable predictors in modeling tasks, as their values do not fluctuate significantly (figure~\ref{fig:fig2}G and E).\par

Features such as Dipole Moment X ($\vec{\mu}_x$) and $\Theta$ value $\Theta$ , however, exhibited substantial variability. The $\vec{\mu}_x$ had a mean of 1.70, but the range of values extended from -3.33 to 7.59, showing that the dipole moment along the X-axis is quite variable. Similarly, the $\Theta$ displayed a large spread, with a mean of 7.30 and a standard deviation of 7.77, indicating significant variation in the dataset. Despite the variability observed in these features, their importance in the models suggests they provide crucial information. The variability in these features may contribute valuable complexity to the model, offering a deeper understanding of the underlying chemical processes, and should be considered in the context of their overall predictive power(figure~\ref{fig:fig2}H and D).\par

Overall, the dataset contains a mix of stable and variable features, which is common in scientific data. While features with narrow distributions like HOMO  and I-O Bond can provide reliable, consistent information, features with wider distributions such as $\vec{\mu}_x$ and $\Theta$ offer a more complex, nuanced perspective on the data. Understanding these distribution patterns is crucial for selecting appropriate modeling techniques, preprocessing strategies, and ensuring that the models can handle both stable and variable features effectively.\par

This energy range suggests that, under the specified experimental conditions, most IBX-mediated reactions are thermodynamically accessible at room temperature, thereby supporting the feasibility of these oxidation processes under mild conditions ~\cite{zhdankin2009hypervalent}.As expected based on the literature, methanol oxidation can be readily achieved at room temperature; however, in this case, the reaction rate differs and may serve as a valuable criterion for comparing reactivity ~\cite{su2005enhancing,jiang2017twist}.\par

The difference in performance between models using DFT descriptors and those using Mordred descriptors can be explained by the distinct types of information they capture. DFT descriptors contain detailed quantum mechanical properties, such as HOMO, LUMO, dipole moments, and specific bond lengths, which are directly related to the electronic structure and energetics of the transition state~\cite{suleimanov2015automated}. This rich information is important for accurately predicting TS barrier energies, as shown by the significantly lower MAEs and higher R² values in all models that use DFT descriptors.\par

In contrast, Mordred descriptors, while advantageous due to their ease of generation from SMILES representations, are primarily based on structural and topological features. These descriptors may not fully capture the subtle electronic and energetic nuances that govern the TS barrier. As a result, models based on Mordred descriptors exhibit poorer predictive performance.\par

The weak correlation observed in the LR and RF models using Mordred descriptors is likely due to a subset of these descriptors having a linear relationship with the TS barrier. LR captures this linear pattern effectively, while the RF’s ensemble method reduces noise, highlighting the relevant signal. As a result, although Mordred descriptors are simpler in structure compared to DFT descriptors, they still provide moderate predictive performance in these models~\cite{reis2018probabilistic}.


According to Tabl~\tabref{1}, the default evaluation results reveal that SVR and the NN models substantially outperform the other methods. Tabl~\tabref{1}  shows that LR achieves an R$^{2}$ of 0.83 with a MAE of approximately 0.95, while ensemble methods like RF and GB achieve improved performance with R$^{2}$ values around 0.94 and lower error metrics. In contrast, both SVR and NN exhibit the highest predictive accuracies, with R$^{2}$ values of 0.974 and 0.975 respectively, accompanied by notably lower MAE and RMSE values. This clearly indicates that models capable of capturing non-linear relationships provide a superior fit for predicting the TS Barrier compared to simpler linear models or ensemble approaches.\par

The superior performance of SVR and NN can be attributed to their inherent ability to model non-linear relationships. SVR uses kernel functions to project data into higher-dimensional spaces where a linear separation becomes feasible, thereby allowing it to capture subtleties in the data that a simple linear model might miss~\cite{roy2023support}. Similarly, NN learn hierarchical feature representations through multiple layers and non-linear activation functions, which makes them highly adaptable to complex patterns inherent in quantum-derived descriptors. In contrast, LR, with its strict linearity assumption, fails to represent these interactions adequately, while ensemble methods, although robust, may not achieve the same level of fine-tuned non-linear modeling as SVR and NN. These findings are consistent with studies in quantitative structure–property relationships, which often report that non-linear models outperform linear ones when the underlying relationships in the data are complex. \par

~\subsection{Buttressing effect in IBX}
The buttressing effect refers to the phenomenon wherein a single bulky substituent induces a twisted conformation in a molecular system, while the addition of a second bulky group further increases steric strain, leading to an enhanced distortion or twisting. In IBX derivatives containing multiple substituents, such as 3-F,5-F-IBX, the substituent at the C3 position continues to increase the $\Theta$ angle (6.76°) while simultaneously reducing the transition state (TS) barrier to 18.58 kcal/mol.

A similar trend is observed with methyl (Me) groups: 3-Me-IBX exhibits a greater $\Theta$ angle (14.28°) and a lower TS barrier (14.88 kcal/mol) compared to its 4-Me analogue (2.96° and 19.85 kcal/mol). The combined substitution of 3-Me and 4-Me in IBX results in an intermediate behavior (13.77° and 18.91 kcal/mol). More notably, the 3,4,5,6-tetramethyl-IBX derivative demonstrates a substantial $\Theta$ increase (23.64°) and a significant reduction in the TS barrier (10.52 kcal/mol), indicating that increased steric bulk effectively lowers the reaction barrier.

This buttressing steric effect is particularly prominent in Category 5 structures, where one functional group is positioned at C3, and another is located at a different site, leading to pronounced steric distortions and reactivity enhancements.

On the other hand, in categry 10 which is related to structures, that have one functional group on carbon 5 and one or more on the  other position except number 3, buttressing steric effect turned-off. in this category we want to know if there was no any functional group on the C3 but we  have more than 1 functional group is  buttressing steric effect remain or not? which it is only happen when one functional group is on the C3. Moreover, based on the results as the more  functional groups , the stronger buttressing steric effect is. 



The electronic properties of substituents influence the reactivity of IBX derivatives. Electron-donating groups (EDGs), such as alkyl, hydroxyl, and amine groups, increase electron density on the aromatic ring, which can decrease the acidity of the carboxylate (position 1) by stabilizing the non-ionized form. Simultaneously, these substituents can reduce the electrophilicity of iodine (position 2), potentially altering its redox behavior, however this is boost first oxidation step which is called ligand exchange. In contrast, electron-withdrawing groups (EWGs), such as nitro, cyano, carboxyl, and sulfonyl groups, withdraw electron density, leading to an increase in carboxylate acidity while making the iodine center more electron-deficient and enhancing its oxidizing ability. The magnitude of these effects is dictated by both inductive (σ-bond) and resonance (π-bond) contributions, with halogens often exhibiting a balance between electron withdrawal through induction and weak donation via resonance. \section{Conclusion}

In this study, we integrated advanced machine learning techniques with high-level computational chemistry to unravel the complex reactivity and substituent effects in hypervalent iodine compounds. We applied various machine learning models, including Linear Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting (GB), Support Vector Regression (SVR), and Neural Networks (NN), to predict the reactivity of each structure using different descriptors. We also used DFT-derived descriptors alongside structural fingerprints and Mordred-derived features separately, aiming to improve prediction accuracy by combining these different feature sets.

Based on our findings, NN and SVR models performed exceptionally well with DFT descriptors, achieving an $R^2$ of approximately 0.97, with NN being slightly superior. In contrast, Mordred-derived features did not yield satisfactory results ($R^2 < 0.3$). Our approach enabled the development of a robust categorization scheme that classifies IBX derivatives into distinct groups based on the number and positions of their substituents.

Given the high accuracy of our models, we analyzed the most important descriptors to identify practical tools for designing new I(V) reagents and explaining the reactivity of existing ones. Our results demonstrate that single-functional group compounds exhibit predictable trends—where substituents at position 3 (ortho to iodine) yield lower transition state (TS) barriers compared to those at positions 4, 5, or 6, aligning with previous experimental findings. Notably, substituent effect analysis revealed that steric factors play a dominant role in modulating reactivity. When bulky groups are positioned at position 3, they significantly increase the dihedral angle ($\Theta$), which in turn lowers the TS barrier and drastically enhances reactivity.

Moreover, our findings indicate:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The size of the bulky group has a more profound influence on reactivity than its electronic nature, highlighting the critical impact of steric hindrance. The introduction of additional categories (9 and 10) further captured the nuanced interplay between substituents. Compounds with a substituent at position 3, in the absence of one at position 5—and vice versa—display distinct reactivity profiles.
    \item A \textbf{buttressing effect} was observed, where a single bulky substituent induces a twisted conformation in the molecular system, while the addition of a second bulky group further increases steric strain, leading to enhanced distortion or twisting.
    \item The combination of bulky functional groups at positions 3 and 4 resulted in a stronger buttressing effect, while adding more functional groups at other positions further amplified reactivity.
    \item The combination of a bulky functional group at position 3 and an electron-donating group at position 5 resulted in a buttressing effect and a positive electronic influence on reactivity.
    \item SHAP analyses corroborated these insights by identifying the dihedral angle and dipole moments as key determinants of the TS barrier. 
   \item These models produced reliable results even with a limited number of descriptors. However, combining DFT and Mordred descriptors did not yield better performance. Fingerprint-based models were useful primarily for predicting reactivity trends rather than providing quantitative insights, as these descriptors are not numerical.
   \item Hyperparameter tuning only improved the GB models, while the default NN and SVR models still outperformed the tuned versions. 
   \end{enumerate}

Collectively, our study provides a comprehensive framework for understanding the interplay of steric and electronic effects in hypervalent iodine chemistry and offers a promising pathway for the rational design of novel reagents that will inform future experimental use and enable insights in our laboratory.
